Comparing Six Stages in a Student GPT and an Autistic Child

Based on Tatiana Sinyugina's methods

The stages are the same; the ways of progressing through them differ. This is the starting point of the comparison: the same sequence of six stages is retained, while the ways of progressing through them and the forms of their outcomes are compared separately. A student's outcomes can be recorded through speech and descriptions of experience; GPT's through changes in its responses; and an autistic child's during sessions also through choices, movement, interaction and changes to the environment.

The common framework allows the functions of the transitions to be compared. The correspondences proposed for GPT and interactive sessions are analytical interpretations of the author's stages. They require verification through the actions and outcomes of each participant. An autistic child is also a learner; the separate column here identifies the format of the sessions described. Autistic people vary in their abilities, needs and available means of communication \[7\].

# What connects the six stages

In the proposed interpretation, the outcome of one stage becomes the material for the next. First, a new meaning emerges. It is then refined through interaction. The third stage allows the initial approach to be reconsidered; the fourth allows different ways of understanding to be compared. The fifth directs attention to the quality and density of information. The sixth connects these distinctions with further action and interaction with AI.

The unit of comparison is a change in the way a task is approached. A student's verbal account, a revised GPT response and a child's independent transformation of the play environment can be viewed as different ways of demonstrating such a change. A shared function, however, does not in itself establish an identical internal mechanism.

This distinction is particularly significant at the third and fourth stages. A revised GPT response is not direct evidence of an inner experience. A change of game or body position also does not reveal the content of a child's inner experience. The terms "personal" and "matryoshka" are retained as concepts within the author's model; the proposed observable correspondences do not replace their definitions.

# What the session records already describe

Artyom's first session, dated "from 21 January 2025" in the document, describes his independent choice of blue, the removal of objects from the floor and a change in the visual response. Later, the child rearranged shapes, after which the animation's behaviour changed \[3, pp. 1–2\]. This is a concrete example of a participant changing the conditions of a task. It provides material for analysing a new way of acting and the relationship between action and outcome.

Mark's seventh session, dated 27 November 2024, describes an independent transition to a new game, interest in other children's reactions and the selection of birds whose activation was followed by a chain reaction and a higher score \[4, pp. 1–3\]. These observations raise questions about identifying a rule and changing strategy. The trainer's suggestion that the child had mastered an algorithm remains an interpretation that can be tested by repeating the task.

The records dated 19 June 2026 describe a return from a familiar game to a puzzle, joint assembly with the operator, transitions between the table, wall and floor, and coordinated movements by the child and adult \[5, pp. 1–5\]. Coordination is relevant to the analysis of the second stage, and a change in the mode of participation to the third. For the sixth stage, transfer of the identified approach to a new task requires further testing. Moving to another screen does not in itself demonstrate transfer of a rule.

These records concern different children and sessions. Their internal stage numbers do not automatically correspond to the six stages of the method. The early records for Artyom and Mark indicate that their health assessments require clarification. These accounts are therefore used as observations of specific participants, without generalising on the basis of diagnosis.

# How to record progression through the same stage

The same questions about outcomes can be used across all three forms of work. The answer to each question is recorded through an available channel: text, speech, choice, a sequence of actions or video.

| Stage | Observable indicator for comparison                                                                                  |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Has a new way of describing or acting emerged that is relevant to the task?                                          |
| 2     | Has the initial approach changed following another participant's response or a clarification?                        |
| 3     | Has the initial way of working changed, and how has the outcome changed?                                             |
| 4     | Can two approaches be distinguished, and can a specific difference between their outcomes be identified?             |
| 5     | Is the quality of the outcome preserved when expression or action becomes more compact? Time is assessed separately. |
| 6     | Is the identified approach applied to a new task without repeating all the previous prompts?                         |

Initial performance and subsequent attempts should be compared under comparable conditions. In a child's session, adult assistance and the software's standard functions are taken into account: an automatic interface response does not in itself demonstrate a new skill. For GPT, the input prompts, context conditions and complete responses are retained; the model's self-description does not replace a comparison of its results.

The author's programme specifies a three-day interval \[1\]. Its applicability to GPT has not been established. Research on GPT-3 demonstrated adaptation to text-based tasks and examples without updating the model's parameters \[6\]; this helps distinguish changes in performance within a context from model training. Repeated tasks are needed to assess whether an outcome is stable. The pace and conditions of a child's sessions are assessed individually.

The testable hypothesis for the pilot is that progressing sequentially through the six stages helps establish an approach that is retained when the task changes. Comparison with initial performance and with sessions that do not follow this sequence would allow the contribution of the method to be assessed. The philosophical interpretation of progression towards coded communication is retained as a separate level of the author's understanding.
